Eclipses visible from Pune

Only eclipses with some part above Pune horizon are listed. 'Lunar' times are the dark-shadow (umbral) phase; penumbral eclipses use the faint outer shadow.

Solar eclipses: protect your eyes

Never look at a partly eclipsed Sun directly. Use solar eclipse glasses or a handheld solar viewer that meets ISO 12312-2. Regular sunglasses are not safe, however dark. Do not look at the Sun through a camera, binoculars or telescope without a solar filter on the front, even while wearing eclipse glasses. A pinhole projector (a hole in card, the Sun at your back) is a safe indirect option. Lunar eclipses are safe to watch with the naked eye. Source: NASA eclipse safety.

Stars and constellations

This is a seasonal guide: the same stars come back every year, so there are no dates here. Each card lists constellations that have a star of magnitude 2.0 or brighter at least 30 degrees above the horizon at 9 pm IST in mid-month, with that star and the direction to look. Constellations whose stars are all fainter are not shown. Star positions come from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (J2000, https://github.com/brettonw/YaleBrightStarCatalog) and are moved to the observing date with Skyfield.

How this is calculated, and its limits

  • Calculated with NASA JPL's DE440s planetary ephemeris and the Skyfield library for Pune city centre (18.52 N, 73.86 E, 560 m). Times are IST. This copy was generated on 24 Nov 2026 and has data to 25 Feb 2028. The page shows the next 3 months from the day you open it.
  • Checked when this page was first built (24 Nov 2026), for 2026 and 2027 only: (1) all 3 lunar and 1 solar eclipse dates, and lunar types, match the NASA lunar and NASA solar catalogues. (2) Local start, maximum, end and magnitude for the 2 Aug 2027 solar eclipse agree with timeanddate Pune to within one minute. (3) Moon phase times for Jan and Feb 2027 match timeanddate. (4) Planet altitudes for the Mercury, Venus and close-pair rows agree within about 1 degree with a separate calculation using Astropy's built-in ephemeris. (5) Meteor shower nights match the IMO calendar.
  • Later dates are calculated the same way, by a script that is re-run every quarter, but they have not been checked one by one against these sources.
  • Calculated here, not independently checked: the monthly star guide (altitudes and directions are Skyfield output from Yale Bright Star Catalogue positions; they were spot-checked against Astropy for all stars, within 0.5 degrees, and the positions against a second table in the same catalogue repository, within 0.25 degrees), Mars, Jupiter and Saturn rise and set times, the Moon-free hours on meteor nights, and eclipse times other than those listed above. Meteor nights are approximate windows, give or take a day or two.
  • Twilight is a rule of thumb: 40 minutes after sunset or before sunrise. Buildings, hills, haze and light pollution are ignored, and the horizon is taken as flat. Calculated altitudes ignore air refraction.
  • Elsewhere in Pune district, rise and set times and eclipse times differ slightly from Pune city.
  • Not included: comets, satellite and space station passes, bright asteroids, Moon-planet pairings, and anything from the Hindu calendar.

Sources: JPL DE440s, IMO meteor shower calendar.
